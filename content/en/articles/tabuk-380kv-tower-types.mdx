A transmission tower does not simply hold conductors above the ground.

It also manages weight, wind, electrical clearance and the mechanical pull created by kilometres of tensioned cable. Change the direction of the route, and those forces change with it. Bring the line to an endpoint, and the structure may have to resist the full tension of the conductor system.

That is why the Tabuk 380 kV transmission line could not be built by repeating one tower design 309 times.

Samaya Group Company Ltd. delivered the double-circuit overhead line across the Tabuk Region for [Saudi Electricity Company](https://www.se.com.sa/en/) between March 2020 and March 2022. The completed route uses suspension, small-angle, medium-angle, heavy-angle, transposition and terminal towers, each assigned to locations where its structural or electrical function was needed.

The differences are easy to miss from the road. They are central to how the line works.

## One route, six structural answers

The tower schedule for the Tabuk project:

| Tower type | Number used |
| --- | --- |
| Suspension towers | 206 |
| Small-angle towers | 33 |
| Medium-angle towers | 23 |
| Heavy-angle towers | 19 |
| Transposition towers | 19 |
| Dead-end or terminal towers | 9 |

Most of the route is carried by suspension towers. That is expected: an efficiently planned transmission alignment usually contains long straight sections, with heavier and more expensive structures reserved for places where the route turns or the conductor system must be anchored.

The numbers also reveal something about the character of the Tabuk corridor.

More than two-thirds of its towers perform the relatively straightforward job of supporting conductors along the route. The remaining structures exist because the line cannot remain mechanically or electrically identical from beginning to end.

## Suspension towers carry the straight sections

Suspension towers are the repeated rhythm of a transmission line.

They support the conductors vertically while allowing the line to continue through relatively straight sections. The conductors pass through suspension-insulator strings rather than being fully anchored at each structure.

Under normal conditions, the pull from one side of the tower is largely balanced by the pull from the other.

That balance allows suspension towers to be lighter than structures used at sharp turns or line endings. It also makes them practical for repeated use across long corridors. The Tabuk project used **206 suspension towers**, making them the dominant structure along the 112.5-kilometre route.

Their apparent simplicity should not be mistaken for a lack of engineering.

Each tower still has to maintain the correct clearance between the conductors, the ground and the structure itself. It must withstand wind, the weight of the conductors and the changing loads created by temperature and operating conditions.

What makes it different is that it does not normally carry the full unbalanced tension of the line.

That changes as soon as the route turns.

## What happens when the line changes direction

A conductor under tension naturally pulls towards a straight line.

When a transmission route changes direction, the tower at the turn must resist conductors pulling from two different angles. The sharper the turn, the greater the sideways force transferred into the structure and its foundation.

The Tabuk line therefore used three categories of angle tower: **33 small-angle**, **23 medium-angle** and **19 heavy-angle** structures.

This is not merely a naming system. Each category represents a different range of mechanical loading. A small-angle tower manages a modest change in direction. A medium-angle structure handles a larger deviation. A heavy-angle tower is designed for the most demanding turns along the route.

As the angle increases, the tower generally requires stronger members, heavier foundations and more robust conductor attachments.

The route therefore influences cost and construction long before tower steel reaches the site.

A straighter alignment may reduce the need for heavy structures, but route design cannot focus on geometry alone. Engineers must also account for land access, settlements, roads, existing utilities, environmental constraints and the clearances required beneath the line.

The final alignment is a compromise between electrical performance, structural demand and what can realistically be built.

## The towers that rearrange electricity

The most unusual structures on the Tabuk route are its **19 transposition towers**.

They do not primarily exist because the route turns. Their purpose is electrical.

A three-phase transmission line carries electricity through three phase conductors. On a tower, those phases occupy different physical positions: one may sit higher, lower, closer to the centre or farther from the other circuits.

Over a long distance, those differences in position can produce small differences in the electrical characteristics of each phase.

A transposition tower changes the physical order of the phases so that, over the complete route, each phase spends time in different positions.

<Figure id="2" />

The objective is balance.

Without transposition, one phase could experience slightly different inductance or capacitance from the others across a long line. Rearranging the positions helps distribute those effects more evenly.

From the ground, a transposition tower may look like another large steel structure. The important difference lies in the path the conductors take through it.

The phase arrangement changes at the tower, but the electrical identity of each phase must remain correct. That requires carefully designed hardware, clearances and installation sequencing.

For the people building the line, this creates a more complex operation than standard suspension stringing. For the network operator, it helps the completed line behave more evenly.

## Where the conductor tension ends

The Tabuk project also included **nine dead-end or terminal towers**.

These structures are used where conductors must be fully anchored rather than simply supported.

At the end of a line section, the conductor does not continue towards another tower with an equal pull from the opposite direction. The terminal structure therefore has to resist the full mechanical tension acting on the system.

Dead-end towers may also be used to divide a long route into sections, or to create stable points around particularly demanding parts of the alignment.

They are typically among the heaviest structures on a transmission line. Their insulator strings are arranged to carry tension rather than suspension loads, and their foundations must be designed for the substantial forces transferred through the tower legs.

Only nine were needed across Tabuk, but the line could not function without them. They provide the restraint that allows the conductors between towers to remain correctly tensioned.

## The route between the towers

The 309 structures created approximately 308 spans across the completed route. On average, the towers stand roughly 365 metres apart, although real spacing changes constantly.

A long open section may allow a wider span. A road crossing, change in elevation or direction shift may require towers to be positioned differently. The height and type of structure also influence what is possible.

The line crossed **17 roads, 11 utility or service corridors and six existing electrical lines** or other special locations.

At those points, route design had to do more than maintain an average spacing.

The conductor needed sufficient clearance above the asset being crossed. The nearby towers had to support the required span and mechanical loads. Construction also had to be planned around traffic, existing services and, where necessary, operational electricity infrastructure.

These crossing points help explain why a transmission line cannot be designed by placing towers at equal intervals on a map. The route is adjusted continuously in response to what lies beneath and around it.

## The conductors dictate the structure

The Tabuk 380 kV line uses a double-circuit, quad-bundle conductor arrangement.

Two circuits means six electrical phases in total. Quad bundle means each phase is carried by four sub-conductors rather than one. That creates 24 conductor paths along the route.

The scheduled quantity was approximately **2,781 kilometres of phase conductor** — far longer than the geographical length of the line, because multiple sub-conductors run in parallel across both circuits.

Every tower type had to support this arrangement. Suspension towers carried the bundles through straight sections. Angle towers redirected them. Transposition structures rearranged the phases. Terminal towers anchored the full system.

The steel structures and conductors were therefore designed as one mechanical arrangement.

A tower cannot be selected independently of the cable it carries. Conductor weight, tension, bundle geometry, span length and wind loading all influence the forces the structure must withstand.

## How the route was delivered

Samaya Group Company Ltd. was responsible for the engineering, procurement and construction of the line. Its scope included route engineering, foundation construction, tower supply and erection, conductor installation, optical ground wire, earthing, protection, communications, testing and commissioning.

The variety of towers added another layer to the construction programme.

Materials had to reach the correct location rather than simply the correct project. A suspension tower package could not replace a heavy-angle structure. A transposition tower required its own conductor arrangement. Terminal structures had to be completed before connected sections could be fully tensioned.

The erection sequence also had to match conductor-stringing plans.

A completed tower is not useful merely because it is standing. It must be the correct structure, installed at the correct position, with the fittings and insulators required for the next stage of work.

By March 2022, the six tower categories, 34 major crossing locations and thousands of kilometres of conductor had been integrated into one operating connection within Saudi Arabia's Northern Grid.

## The line does not repeat itself

From a distance, transmission towers can appear almost interchangeable.

The Tabuk project shows why they are not.

Some structures carry balanced loads through straight sections. Others resist the sideways pull created by a turn. Nineteen rearrange the physical positions of the electrical phases. Nine provide the fixed points against which the conductor system is tensioned.

Together, the 309 towers record the route in steel.

Every straight section, every turn, every electrical adjustment and every endpoint is reflected in the structure selected for that location.

The line may run continuously for 112.5 kilometres, but it does not simply repeat itself.
